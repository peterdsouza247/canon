Canon
changes the wording, not the numbers

Can this crew member fly this flight?
And can you prove why.

A rules engine that can tell you
what it needs, and what it did.

Every time a planner puts a crew member on a flight, something has to decide whether that is legal. Rest, hours flown, licences, who else is on board. Airlines run those checks through a rules system.

Canon is a replacement for that system. It is built around three things the current one cannot do: work out for itself what information it needs, show its working for every answer, and tell you exactly when a rule last changed and who changed it.

Crew rostering legality, without the licence fee. Canon derives its own payload contract from the rules by static analysis, refuses undeclared coupling between rules, explains every decision at rule granularity, and keeps a tamper evident record of what was deployed when.

Everything below is worked out live in your browser from the actual rules and the actual data. Nothing here is a screenshot and no figure is typed in by hand. Change the settings and the numbers change with them.

Independent project. Not affiliated with, endorsed by, or derived from any employer's systems. Built from public sources. Every rule, operator, flight, aircraft and person on this page is invented; the duty and rest limits are shaped like published regulation and are not a copy of it.

One decision, start to finish

It helps to have a specific case in mind. Here is one, and here is where the current setup struggles with it.

Step 1

A planner puts Captain Okonjo on flight ZQ4207 tomorrow morning. Five sectors, a long day, and she has not adjusted to the local time zone yet.

Step 2 · problem

The rostering system sends the rules everything it holds: her whole crew record, the duty plan, the flight, every other crew member on board. Most of it is never looked at, and it all has to travel.

Step 3

The rules answer. In this case: not legal. The duty is 14 and a half hours and the most she is allowed today is 11.

Step 4 · problem

Six months later somebody asks why that roster was built the way it was. Which rule said so? Was it the same rule we have now? Nobody can say.

What Canon changes

Step 2 gets smaller, because Canon works out from the rules themselves exactly which facts are needed and asks only for those. Step 4 becomes a lookup, because every answer keeps a record of what it looked at and which rule decided, and every rule change is logged in a way nobody can quietly edit.

What it was built against Seven recurring problems, and what each one turns into

This was not designed as a general purpose product. It was designed against the set of problems that long-lived rules systems tend to accumulate. Anyone who has maintained one for a decade will recognise them.

This is not a general theory of rules engines. It is a response to the failure modes that long lived business rules deployments reliably develop.

The recurring problemWhat it becomes
1 The message sent to the rules is large and getting larger, and it shows in how long a roster takes to build Canon reads its own rules and works out the short list of facts they actually use. Everything else stops being sent. A projection derived by static analysis of the rules. It contains what the rules read, and nothing else.
2 The way it is built makes it hard to send only what is needed Nobody keeps that short list up to date, because nobody writes it. It is produced from the rules every time, so it cannot fall out of step with them. Nobody maintains the field list. It is generated, so it cannot drift from the rules.
3 The data arrives shaped the wrong way: everything about one person, when some rules need one detail about everybody on the flight A rule can simply say "the rank of everyone on this flight" and that is a normal request, fetched in one go. It stops being a special case people work around. Collection traversal is part of the path grammar. flight.roster[*].rank is an ordinary fact path.
4 Rules quietly depend on other rules, and the people writing rules are not in step with the people wiring them up If a rule uses another rule's answer it has to say so, in writing, in the rule. The system checks the claim, refuses circular ones, and groups rules into rounds that cannot see each other. Coupling must be declared, is checked against the syntax tree, is refused if cyclic, and is sorted into strata that provably cannot see one another.
5 Commercial rules engines are expensive to licence This is a small piece of software your own team owns, with no licence and no vendor. A Python library with no runtime dependencies outside the standard library.
6 When something looks wrong, finding the rule responsible is hard Every answer comes with a receipt: which rules were considered, which were skipped and why, what each one looked at, and what it concluded. Press "why" on any result below. Every decision carries a trace. decision.explain(code) walks it backwards through the derivations.
7 Working out which release broke a rule is hard Every rule has a fingerprint, every release records them all, and every deployment is logged in a chain that shows if anyone edits the past. One command names the release, the date and the person. Content hash per rule, manifest per release, hash chained ledger per environment. One command names the release, the date and the person.

The rounds of checking, worked out from the rules on this page

Canon reads the rules and sorts them into rounds. Round one is everything that can be worked out from the raw facts. Round two is everything that needs an answer from round one, and so on. Within a round, no rule can see any other rule's answer, so nothing depends on the order they happen to run in.

Who writes the rules One rule, three ways to write it

A rules system is only as good as the people who can safely change it. The same rule is shown three ways below. All three end up identical inside the engine, so this is a choice about who does the writing and the reviewing, not about what the software does.

The short recommendation

Spreadsheet-style tables for the licence and qualification checks, because there are dozens of them, they all look the same, and non-programmers should be able to read and change them. Structured text for the fatigue rules, because there are fewer of them, they involve real arithmetic, and they need to be read carefully in review. Program code only where it earns its place.

All three compile to the same rule object and produce the same content hash, so the choice is about who writes and reviews rules, not about what the engine does. The trade offs, including where each one hurts, are in docs/authoring-comparison.md. 



    

      

        
Structured text

        

          Reads almost like a form. A rostering manager can follow it in review even if they would not want to write one from scratch. Any dependency on another rule has to be written down, which is the point.
          Readable in review by people who do not write code. Coupling between rules is mandatory to declare. Recommended for the flight time limitation and composition rules, which are few, intricate and interdependent.
        

      

      

        
Program code

        

          Best tools for a developer, and never actually run: Canon reads the code and converts it. Only a very narrow shape is allowed. No "otherwise" branches, because two possible outcomes should be two separate rules that can be switched off independently.
          Full editor support. Compiled from the syntax tree and never executed. Accepts a narrow shape only: a docstring, then one if, then emits and sets. No else, because two outcomes are two rules.
        

      

      

        
Spreadsheet table

        

          One row per rule, one column per condition. Closest to how the business already thinks about qualification matrices, and the only one a non-programmer can confidently edit. The risk: an empty cell means "no condition here", and an empty cell looks like nothing at all.
          Closest to how business users of a commercial BRMS already think, and the best fit for the qualification matrices. Watch the ratio of = escape hatch cells. Past about one in ten, those rows want moving to YAML.

      Problem 7
      Which release changed this rule, and can anyone edit the record
      Tamper evidence: which deployment changed this rule
    


    

      

        Every rule gets a fingerprint calculated from what it says. Comments and titles
        are left out on purpose, so tidying up the wording of a rule does not make it
        look like the rule changed. Every release records the fingerprints of all its
        rules. Every deployment is written into a logbook where each entry contains a
        summary of the one before it.
      

      

        That last part is what makes the logbook hard to falsify. Editing a past entry
        breaks the chain, and the break is visible immediately. Press the red button
        below and watch it happen. The fingerprints are real, calculated in your browser.
      

    


    

      Every rule has a content hash over its semantics only, so editing a comment does
      not churn the diff. Every release is a manifest of those hashes with a Merkle
      root. Every deployment is an entry that carries the previous entry's hash. The
      hashes below are real SHA-256, computed in your browser.

The words on this page

    

      A rules engine attracts jargon. Here is the short version of each term, in the
      sense it is used above.
    

    

      

        
Rule

        
One check, with one outcome. "Rest before duty must be at least twelve hours."
          Deliberately small, so it can be switched off, versioned and blamed on its own.

      

      

        
Finding also: violation

        
What a rule produces when it triggers. Either hard, meaning the assignment is
          not legal, or soft, meaning it is legal but somebody should look at it.

      

      

        
Fact

        
One piece of information a rule can look at. A crew member's hours last month,
          a flight's departure time, whether the destination needs a special briefing.

      

      

        
Payload also: the message

        
Everything the rostering system sends over to the rules for one decision.
          Today it contains far more than the rules use, and that is problem one.

      

      

        
Contract technically: projection

        
The list of facts the rules actually need. Canon produces it by reading its
          own rules, so it is never out of date and nobody maintains it by hand.

      

      

        
Vertical slice

        
One detail about every person on a flight, rather than everything about one
          person. "The rank of everyone on board." Some rules cannot be written without it.

      

      

        
Working figure technically: derived value

        
A number one rule calculates for other rules to use, like the maximum duty
          length allowed today. A rule that uses one has to say so in writing.

      

      

        
Round technically: stratum

        
A group of rules that cannot see each other's answers. Everything in round one
          runs first, then round two, and inside a round the order never matters.

      

      

        
Trace the working, the receipt

        
The record kept for every decision: which rules were considered, which were
          skipped and why, what each looked at, what each concluded.

      

      

        
Fingerprint technically: content hash

        
A short code calculated from what a rule says. Change the rule and the code
          changes. Change only a comment and it does not.

      

      

        
Shadow run

        
Running the new system alongside the old one on real traffic, throwing the new
          answer away, and recording every disagreement.

      

      

        
What-if replay

        
Taking a proposed rule change, running it against decisions already made, and
          counting how many would come out differently.

      

    

  






  

    
What this deliberately will not do

    

      A rules system is as much about what it refuses as what it allows. Every refusal below is a decision somebody could disagree with, so they are listed rather than buried.
      A rules engine is as much about what it refuses as what it allows.
    


    

      

        
Refused by design

        

          "otherwise" branches, loops, scratch variables inside a rule
          else, elif, loops, local variables in a rule
          two outcomes are two rules
        

        

          a rule asking what today's date is
          now(), random, anything non deterministic
          the date is supplied as a fact
        

        

          rule order changing the answer
          priority affecting logic
          it orders output only
        

        

          arbitrary program code inside a rule
          eval, exec, imports, lambdas, f-strings
          a small fixed vocabulary
        

        

          using another rule's answer without saying so
          reading a derived fact without declaring it
          refuses to load
        

        

          claiming a dependency you do not actually use
          declaring a read you do not use
          also refuses to load
        

        

          two rules setting the same figure with no tie-break
          two rules writing one value with no policy
          refuses to load
        

        

          rules that depend on each other in a circle
          a dependency cycle
          refuses to load, naming them
        

      

      

        
Honest limits

        

          Canon checks every rule that applies, one at a time. For a few hundred rules answering one question at a time that is the right trade, and it is why the engine is small enough to read in a sitting. A rulebook in the tens of thousands, with lots of shared conditions, would want a different design.
          There is no RETE network and no incremental matching. For a per assignment legality check over a few hundred rules that is the right trade, and it is why the engine is small enough to read in a sitting. For a ruleset in the tens of thousands with heavily shared subconditions, a matching network would win.
        

        

          The converter that reads rules out of a commercial engine handles the common shapes and refuses the awkward ones rather than guessing. On a real estate of rules, expect a genuine pile of manual work left over. Guessing would be worse.
          The importer for business action language handles the common shapes and refuses collection quantifiers and unbracketed mixed precedence rather than guessing. Expect a real manual residue on a real estate of rules.
        

        

          The logbook proves nobody has edited the past, but the current signing method means anyone who can check a signature could also forge one. For a regulator-facing record that needs upgrading, and the upgrade is well understood.
          Signing uses HMAC from the standard library, which means anybody who can verify can also forge. A regulated deployment wants asymmetric signatures with the key in a KMS, and the chain head published where the rules team cannot rewrite it.
        

        

          The duty and rest numbers used throughout this demo are made up. They are shaped like a real regulation. They are not one, and nothing should be flown on them.
          The flight time limitation numbers in the examples are illustrative. They are shaped like a real regulation. They are not one.
